Date: 2026-08-29
Purpose: Ground a wall-tile patterning/takeoff feature in real tile-setting layout practice, especially how tiled fields are handled at corners.

## Q1 — How a wall of tile is laid out (reference lines, balancing, origin)

**Working / reference lines.** A wall layout is built on two struck reference lines:
- a **level horizontal line** (a "ledger" or **batten** line), and
- a **plumb vertical line** (the vertical centerline / offset line).

The horizontal control is established by finding the **lowest point** along the run, striking a **level** line one tile-height up from it, and screwing a straight **batten/ledger board (a 1×2)** into the studs on that line. Tile is set upward off the batten so the grid stays level and square even when the floor or tub deck is not; the batten is removed later and the bottom (cut) course is filled in last. [TRADE — This Old House, "Tile Setting 101"; DIYTileGuy] The vertical control is a **plumb centerline** struck with a level or laser; for offset/running-bond a **second vertical line** is struck a defined distance from the first to carry the stagger (DIYTileGuy spaces the two lines ~3 in for 3×6 subway to define the running-bond offset, and 9–15 in apart to keep layout marks clear of morter). [TRADE — DIYTileGuy]

**Balancing the field (the core rule).** Center and balance so edge cuts are equal on opposing sides and **no cut is smaller than half a tile**:
- ANSI A108.02 §4.3 directs installers to "**Center and balance areas of tile, if possible**," and states "**An excessive amount of cuts shall not be made. Usually, no cuts smaller than half size should be made.**" (quoted via CTEF) [EDU — CTEF]
- The same rule appears in CSI 09 30 13 specs: "tile work laid out and centered in both directions in each space or on each wall area, minimizing the use of pieces that are less than half of a tile." [STD — CSI 09 30 13, VA/institutional masterspecs]
- Cut tiles belong "on the outer edges of the field" where trim, molding, or sealant conceals them — not scattered through the field. [EDU — CTEF]

**Choosing the origin.** The vertical centerline is a *starting hypothesis*, not the final origin: dry-lay a course with spacers, and if either end lands on a sliver, **shift the layout by up to a half tile** so both ends get a decent cut. The commonly-criticized wrong method is starting with a full tile hard against one wall and accepting whatever sliver falls at the far wall. [EDU — CTEF; TRADE — DIYTileGuy, running-bond forum threads] Vertically, the field is balanced too: full tiles start at the batten line, cut courses land at floor and ceiling/top-of-wainscot.

Net: a wall's layout has **two independent balancing axes** — horizontal course position (set by the plumb centerline, shiftable to balance end cuts) and vertical course position (set by the level batten). This two-axis structure is what makes the corner question below subtle.

## Q2 — Corners: how a tiled field turns onto an adjacent wall (THE key question)

### First, a geometric correction to the three-model framing

The brief's three models — (a) wrap/continuous, (b) reset-inside/wrap-outside, (c) reset-every-corner — conflate two *different* continuities that behave oppositely at inside vs outside corners:

1. **Horizontal course-height continuity** (are the grout-line heights the same on both walls?).
2. **Offset-phase / field-position continuity** (does the running-bond stagger and the left/right position of the vertical joints carry through, or restart?).

Course height is governed by the **level batten**, and a level line is level everywhere in the room. So on any two walls meeting at an **inside** corner, the horizontal grout lines line up **automatically and non-optionally** — a setter does not get to "reset" them without deliberately making the wall un-level. What is actually carried-or-reset is the **offset phase and the horizontal field position** (which vertical joint lands where). Sources bear this out: setters continue the *pattern* around inside corners specifically by taking the **offcut** from the wall they just finished and starting the next wall with it, so "the cuts would be exactly like the subway tile pattern continues." [TRADE — DIYTileGuy]

Because of that, the brief's model **(b) "reset inside corners but wrap outside corners" is backwards relative to the geometry**: inside corners are the *easy* wrap (you cut into the corner and carry the offcut onto the next wall), whereas **outside corners are the ones necessarily interrupted** — the tile face has to be terminated with a miter, a bullnose, or a metal profile before the adjacent face begins. This mismatch is flagged rather than papered over. No authoritative source describes a "reset-inside / wrap-outside" practice.

### Mapping the three models to real practice

**(a) WRAP / continuous — the common default for the primary field.**
- **Inside corners:** Standard practice is to **carry the pattern around**. Center the most-seen wall, cut into the inside corner, and **reuse the cut-off piece to start the adjacent wall**, so the running-bond/subway pattern reads as folding around the corner. Physically each wall is still set one-at-a-time (finish and let one wall set before the abutting wall, so the corner tiles don't slump), but the *layout* is continuous. [TRADE — DIYTileGuy; Bay Tile; Level Up Tiling] Course heights continue automatically (level batten, above).
- **Outside corners:** the field "wraps" only in the sense that the pattern continues onto the return face; the **edge itself must be finished** because two glazed tile faces cannot simply meet at 90°. Options: **mitered tile edges (45°)**, a **bullnose tile** that turns the corner, or a **metal/PVC edge profile** (see Q3). [TRADE — HowToSpecialist; Bay Tile / Daltile on bullnose]

**(b) RESET inside / WRAP outside — not a recognized convention (see correction above).** No authoritative source describes this. It is listed only to record that it was looked for and not found.

**(c) RESET every corner — used when balance beats continuity, and for some patterns.**
- Each wall gets its **own centered, balanced layout from its own centerline**, so every wall independently avoids slivers — at the cost of the joints not aligning across the corner. This is the fallback when carrying the offcut would force an ugly sliver on the next wall, and it is implied by the ANSI "center and balance … in each space or **on each wall area**" language, which authorizes per-wall balancing. [STD — CSI 09 30 13; EDU — CTEF] It is also the norm where walls are treated as separate design surfaces (e.g., a feature wall vs. its neighbors) or where the corner is broken by trim anyway.

### Which is standard, and what it depends on

There is **no single universal rule**; practice is driven by which continuity the eye will notice:
- **Inside corners, running-bond/subway/grid:** *wrap by default* (carry the offcut). Reset only if wrapping throws a sliver onto the next wall — then reset-and-balance that wall.
- **Outside corners:** always physically interrupted by a finished edge (miter / bullnose / profile); the pattern may still be visually continued onto the return.
- **Depends on:** inside-vs-outside (biggest factor), whether opposing end cuts stay ≥ half tile, tile type (rectified/large-format and stone favor mitered or profiled edges; small glazed field tile tolerates bullnose), and pattern (below).

**Honest gaps:** the *precedence rule* when "wrap the pattern" and "avoid slivers on the next wall" conflict is a matter of setter judgment and design intent; sources describe both outcomes and none gives a hard priority. Authoritative standards (ANSI/TCNA/NTCA) specify **centering/balancing and no-sub-half-cuts**, and require expansion/movement joints at inside corners and changes of plane, but they do **not** prescribe whether the offset phase must be carried around a corner. That is a design/aesthetic decision, and the standards are effectively silent on it.

## Q3 — Corner-specific materials that get counted, and when

Trim is counted **per finished edge and per corner**, and it is what an outside corner or an exposed field edge needs instead of a raw tile edge.

- **Bullnose tile** — a field tile with one rounded, finished edge; used to finish **exposed edges and outside corners**, top of a wainscot/backsplash, and as a tiled base. **Double bullnose** (two adjacent finished edges) is used where both a top and a side edge show, e.g., the end of a half-tiled wall. [MFR — Daltile, "All About Bullnose Tile"] Counting unit: **each (EA)**, one per corner/edge tile position; a wall's exposed vertical edge needs one bullnose per course.
- **Trim / corner shapes in the spec sense** — CSI 09 30 13 submittals explicitly call for "**Trim shapes, bullnose cap and cove including bullnose cap and base pieces at internal and external corners of vertical surfaces**." So **both inside (internal) and outside (external) corners of vertical surfaces get dedicated corner pieces** counted by type/size. Cove and cove-base corner pieces (inside corners at floor/wall junctions) are their own count. [STD — CSI 09 30 13, VA masterspec]
- **Metal / PVC edge profiles (Schluter-type)** — for outside corners and exposed edges as an alternative to bullnose/miter:
  - **RONDEC** (rounded reveal), **QUADEC** (squared reveal), **JOLLY** (single 90° edge), **FINEC/FINEC-SQ**, **INDEC**, **DECO-DE** (135°) — all listed by Schluter under "Edging & Outside Wall Corners." Sold **by length (linear foot / metre)**; standard lengths ~8'2½" (250 cm) and ~9'10" (300 cm). Matching pre-formed **outside-corner connector pieces** exist for some profiles and are counted **each**. [MFR — Schluter, Edging & Outside Wall Corners]
  - **Inside corners / change-of-plane movement joints** use a different family (**DILEX-HK / DILEX-PHK** cove-shaped movement profiles). Note: Schluter's *outside-corner* page does **not** list these — the DILEX family is the inside-corner/movement-joint line — so this is a category distinction to preserve in any takeoff. Counting unit: **linear foot** along the inside corner.

**Takeaway for a takeoff/counting model:** profiles = **LF** (times a wall's edge/corner height); bullnose and pre-formed corner pieces = **EA** (per corner tile or per corner). Outside corners and every exposed field edge generate trim; inside corners generate either a cove/inside-corner piece or a movement-joint profile, both **LF**.

## Q4 — Patterns across walls (running bond, herringbone, basketweave, etc.)

- **Running bond / offset (incl. subway):** carried around **inside** corners via the offcut (Q2). The **offset direction** is set by the two struck vertical lines; the stagger continues onto the next wall when you start it with the carried offcut. Reset per wall only to protect end-cut balance. [TRADE — DIYTileGuy]
- **Grid / straight-set:** the CSI default is a grid "unless otherwise indicated," centered both directions per wall. Grid lines are the easiest to continue around an inside corner because both axes are already controlled by the batten and centerline. [STD — CSI 09 30 13]
- **Herringbone (and diagonal 45° layouts):** built off a **center reference line** for symmetry; every perimeter piece (and thus every corner piece) is a **diagonal cut**. Sources describe herringbone as started from the center of the wall/space and do **not** describe carrying the exact zig-zag phase around a corner; in practice the diagonal geometry means corners are individually cut and the pattern is generally **reset/re-centered per surface** rather than phase-matched across a corner. Sources are effectively **silent** on a formal cross-corner rule for herringbone, so this is stated as observed practice, not a codified rule. [TRADE — RUBI, Mineral Tiles, Mercury Mosaics herringbone guides]
- **Basketweave / mosaic sheets:** typically sheet-mounted modules; corners are cut through the sheet and the module simply continues or restarts at the sheet grid. No authoritative cross-corner rule found — **silent**.

**Pattern-specific rule that IS codified:** none of the standards mandate an offset *direction* at corners. What they mandate is balance and no sub-half cuts (Q1) and movement joints at corners/changes of plane. Offset carry-through is aesthetic.

## Q5 — How patterns are sketched / specified (elevations & shop drawings)

Wall tile is specified on **tile shop drawings**, which include **elevation views** (side/vertical views of each wall) in addition to plan layouts.

Per CSI 09 30 13 and shop-drawing practice, a wall-tile **elevation / shop drawing conventionally shows** [STD — CSI 09 30 13, VA & institutional masterspecs; TRADE — US Drafting, McLine shop-drawing guides]:
- **Overall layout and the reference/centerlines** — where full tiles start and the starting point / origin.
- **The pattern** (grid, running bond, herringbone, etc.) and **joint locations / joint alignment**, with joint width (typ. **1/16" wall, 1/8" floor** per CSI).
- **Extent and limits** — full-height vs **wainscot** height, borders, accent bands.
- **Dimensions of each area**, including **corners, transitions, and irregular surfaces**, and where cut tiles fall.
- **Perimeter conditions and junctions with dissimilar materials**, thresholds.
- **Trim components** (bullnose/cap/base/corner pieces) and their locations.
- **Control / expansion (movement) joints** — the spec writer note in the VA masterspec directs: "Detail wall, floor, and base edge, joints with other materials, and expansion joint conditions … **Show locations of expansion joints on drawings**."
- **Setting details** at corners and edges.

Conceptually the pattern is captured as a **repeat unit + reference lines**: a repeat (e.g., the two-tile running-bond unit, or the herringbone module) tiled off a struck centerline/batten across an elevation, with corner and perimeter cuts dimensioned. This maps cleanly onto a feature that stores a repeat unit and per-wall reference lines and renders each wall as an elevation. [TRADE — US Drafting Inc., McLine Studios shop-drawing guides]

## Q6 — Waste / cut accounting at corners and wall ends

**Standards are silent on a numeric overage figure.** ANSI/TCNA/NTCA prescribe *layout quality* (center/balance, no sub-half cuts, cuts to the perimeter) but do **not** publish a "buy X% extra" rule. CSI specs handle spares via **attic-stock / extra-materials** clauses (a named quantity or %, project-specific), not a layout waste factor.

The widely-repeated figures — **~10% straight/grid, ~15% diagonal, ~18–20% herringbone** — trace **only to non-authoritative SEO calculator sites**, which largely cite each other. They are recorded here **as common trade rules of thumb, not as association-backed numbers**, exactly because a findings doc that claims to prefer authoritative sources should not launder aggregator math as standard. [AGG — makecalcs, tilecalcs, homedecorcalc, tileprocalculator, herron; no [STD]/[MFR]/[EDU] source found for these percentages]

**Directionally reliable, source-supported points:**
- Waste rises with **cut density**: more perimeter, corners, alcoves, and diagonal/herringbone geometry → more cuts and more unusable offcuts. [AGG, but internally consistent and physically obvious]
- **Reusing offcuts around inside corners** (carry the cut-off to the next wall) genuinely reduces waste and is why the "wrap" method is also the economical one. [TRADE — DIYTileGuy]
- **Outside corners** consume extra material specifically through **mitering** (a spoiled miter is scrap) or through **dedicated bullnose/corner/profile pieces** counted separately from field tile (Q3).

**Recommendation for the feature:** do not hard-code the 10/15/18% numbers as if authoritative. Either expose them as user-editable trade defaults labeled as rules of thumb, or derive waste from the actual computed perimeter-cut count per wall. Count corner trim (bullnose EA / profile LF) as line items distinct from field-tile waste.

## Consolidated corner-model verdict (for the return summary)

| Model | Real practice | Default? |
|---|---|---|
| (a) Wrap / continuous | Inside corners: carry the offcut so the pattern folds around; course heights continue automatically off the level batten. Outside corners: pattern may continue but the edge is always finished (miter/bullnose/profile). | **Yes — default for the primary field at inside corners.** |
| (b) Reset inside / wrap outside | Not a recognized convention; geometrically backwards (inside wraps easily, outside is the interrupted one). Not found in any authoritative source. | No. |
| (c) Reset every corner | Each wall balanced from its own centerline; joints don't cross the corner. Fallback when wrapping would throw a sliver, and the norm for feature-wall / per-surface designs and often for herringbone/diagonal. | Situational fallback. |

Standards mandate **center/balance + no sub-half cuts + movement joints at corners**; they are **silent** on whether the offset phase must carry around a corner — that is a design decision.
